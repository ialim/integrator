# Product Brief: integrator *(working title)*

## Executive Summary

integrator is a B2B wholesale distribution and project-design platform built by a systems integrator, for systems integrators — launching in Nigeria, with West Africa as the next phase. Today, integrators across IT, AV, security, and home-automation trades design a client's project, source components from scattered suppliers, work out their own markup by hand, and hunt down installation documentation, all across disconnected tools and relationships. integrator collapses that into one place: a wholesale catalogue aggregated from multiple suppliers, a room-by-room project designer that builds a bill of materials directly from that catalogue, a client-facing proposal the integrator shares with their own margin already built in, and installation/solution documentation tied to every product — plus a direct-buy path for integrators who just need to order parts without designing a project first.

This fragmentation is a known, unsolved problem even in mature markets: distributors, design/quoting tools, and documentation are typically separate products stitched together through integrations, not a native single platform. In Nigeria and West Africa specifically, the gap is starker — no incumbent platform currently serves systems integrators as a wholesale distribution channel at all, despite active, fragmented demand across residential and commercial IT, AV, security, and home-automation work.

integrator starts as a distributor: buy from suppliers, mark up, sell wholesale to integrators — margin-on-goods is the entire revenue model, with no separate platform fee or subscription. The founder's own existing supplier relationships and integrator network seed both sides of the marketplace before it has to survive as a cold start. Success in year one is judged less by signups and more by repeat-purchase rate — whether integrators come back to buy again, the real signal that the catalogue, pricing, and experience are good enough to become someone's default way of sourcing a job.

## The Problem

Today, a systems integrator running an AV, security, IT, or home-automation job in Nigeria or West Africa juggles the same job across four or five disconnected surfaces: a spreadsheet or paper quote to design the project room by room, WhatsApp calls or visits to multiple suppliers to check pricing and availability, a separate calculation (or head math) to work out their own markup before quoting the client, a manual proposal built in Word or Excel, and whatever the manufacturer happened to publish for installation guidance — if anything. There is no single source of truth for what's in stock, what it costs wholesale, or what it should cost the client.

The cost shows up in concrete ways: margin errors from doing markup math by hand under time pressure; hours lost chasing multiple suppliers for a price and a lead time instead of running the job; clients who wait days for a proposal that should take an hour; installs that go wrong or take longer because the integrator improvises without proper documentation; and no way to earn better pricing as volume grows, because there's no loyalty mechanism tied to real purchase history.

Regionally, the consequences are sharper. Currency volatility (the naira has moved from roughly ₦460/$1 to ₦1,330–1,430/$1 in two years) means a supplier quoting in naira today can be underwater tomorrow if they don't reprice deliberately. Counterfeit product is a documented risk in several categories, and there's no trusted, verified sourcing channel serving this trade — buyers currently rely on personal relationships and reputation rather than a platform that stands behind what it sells. And because no unified platform serves this vertical here, every integrator solves it alone.

## The Solution

integrator gives a systems integrator one place to go from "what does this project need" to "the client has a proposal and the order is on its way."

It starts with a wholesale catalogue aggregated from multiple suppliers — one place to see what's available and at what price, rather than working the phones across several contacts. From there, an integrator designs the job room by room, pulling products directly from that catalogue into a bill of materials for the project. When the design is ready, they set their own margin on top of the wholesale price, and the platform turns that into a client-facing proposal they can share directly — the client sees a clean, priced proposal; the integrator's cost and margin stay theirs. At this stage the proposal is a formal quote only: nothing is reserved with suppliers and no order exists yet. Client-side payment happens entirely off-platform, however the integrator and their client normally handle it — the order itself only becomes real once the integrator pays for it, which is what protects the platform from committing stock or placing supplier orders against business that hasn't actually closed.

Not every job needs a full design pass — an integrator who just needs to restock parts or fulfil a small order can skip straight to a direct-buy flow against the same catalogue.

Every product and packaged solution carries the installation and configuration documentation an integrator needs to do the job right the first time, closing the gap between "what did I just buy" and "how do I actually install this."

Because integrator is also managing real supply-chain and currency risk as a wholesale distributor in a volatile-currency market, pricing is actively managed rather than fixed — repriced deliberately against currency movement instead of eroding margin silently. Two mechanics extend that relationship over time: a loyalty program that gives better wholesale pricing based on an integrator's trailing spend (so pricing can move up or down with actual buying activity, not a one-time perk), and a back-order option — at a modest discount — for catalog items not currently held in stock, so the catalogue can be wider than the warehouse without either side losing the sale.

## What Makes This Different

The real differentiator here isn't a technical moat — it's that nobody has put these pieces (multi-supplier catalogue, room-by-room design, margin-aware client proposals, order confirmation gated on payment, install documentation, loyalty pricing) into one native platform for this trade, anywhere, and nobody currently serves this trade as a wholesale channel at all in Nigeria or West Africa. Globally, integrators live with distribution, design/quoting, and documentation as separate tools glued together by integrations. Regionally, there's no incumbent to unseat — the same fragmentation FMCG B2B platforms (TradeDepot, Omnibiz) have already proven integrators-in-the-broad-sense will pay to solve, applied here for the first time.

The founder is a practicing integrator, not an outside entrant guessing at the trade's needs — the catalogue, the workflow, and the first customers all start from real relationships and real credibility in the trade, not a cold launch. That's an advantage of trust and access, not a defensible technology moat, and it should be treated as such: it buys time and a real starting position, not permanent protection once the model is proven and others notice the gap.

Two structural choices reinforce the position over time rather than being one-off features: pricing that's actively managed against currency volatility instead of fixed and silently eroding, and loyalty pricing tied to trailing purchase activity, which gives integrators a reason to consolidate their buying here rather than split it across several suppliers — the more of their volume runs through *integrator*, the better their pricing gets, which is the flywheel the business actually depends on.

## Who This Serves

The primary user is a systems integrator in Nigeria or West Africa running IT, AV, security, or home-automation jobs — typically a small outfit, from a solo operator to a handful of technicians, currently juggling supplier relationships, pricing, and documentation by hand. Picture a three-person AV and security integration business in Lagos: today they call two or three suppliers to price a job, build a quote in Excel, mark it up from memory or a rough rule of thumb, and hope the installation goes smoothly because the only documentation they have is whatever came in the box. What they need is speed and reliability — see the real wholesale price and availability once, design the job against it, quote the client without doing the markup math by hand, and have the install go right the first time. Success for them looks like less time spent chasing suppliers, fewer pricing mistakes, and better wholesale pricing over time as they keep buying through the platform.

A secondary beneficiary is the supplier side of the catalogue: manufacturers and distributors who want reach into this integrator community without building and maintaining their own dealer network from scratch — *integrator* becomes their route to market. The end client (the integrator's own customer) is not a platform user in this version — they only ever see the proposal the integrator shares with them — but they're the one who ultimately benefits from an integrator getting a faster, more accurate quote and a properly documented, professionally installed job.

## Success Criteria

The single clearest signal that this is working: repeat-purchase rate — integrators who buy once and come back to buy again, not just integrators who sign up. Sign-ups and one-time orders are easy to get with the right introductory offer; a second and third order means the catalogue, pricing, and experience are genuinely better than what they were doing before.

Alongside that, a small set of measures round out the picture:
- **Active buying integrators** (placed at least one order in the trailing window) — the count of the base actually transacting, not just registered
- **Order value mix between designed-project orders and direct-buy orders** — tells you whether the room-by-room design tool is actually being used, or whether integrator is functioning as a simpler direct-buy store
- **Realized margin held against currency movement** — whether the active-repricing approach is actually protecting margin as the naira moves, not just a stated intention
- **Catalogue breadth** (number of suppliers and SKUs live) — since the model depends on the catalogue being genuinely useful, not thin

These are the numbers to watch first; concrete targets get set once sprint planning/PRD defines them.

## Scope

**In for v1:**
- Wholesale catalogue seeded from the founder's own existing supplier contacts, scoped to IT, AV, security, and home-automation products (residential + light commercial)
- Room-by-room project designer that builds a bill of materials directly from the catalogue
- Margin-aware, shareable client-facing proposals (integrator sets margin; client sees a clean priced quote)
- Order confirmation gated on integrator payment — no supplier order or stock commitment before that
- Direct-buy flow that skips the design step entirely
- Installation/configuration documentation attached to catalogue products
- Loyalty discount tiers based on trailing-window purchase spend
- Discounted back-order flow for catalogue items not held in stock
- Actively-managed, currency-aware pricing (deliberate repricing against naira volatility, not fixed pricing)
- Nigeria as the only launch market

**Explicitly out for v1:**
- Industrial automation and complex commercial projects (multi-resident buildings, hotels, office complexes) — long-term vision, not this version
- End-client accounts or any B2C surface, including the "hire an integrator through the platform" idea — parked to the addendum as future direction
- On-site room-measurement tooling (AR or otherwise) — not required
- Any platform fee, commission, or subscription — revenue is margin-on-goods only
- Formal trade-credit/financing products (e.g. BNPL) — a known regional pattern, but not part of this version's ask
- Self-service supplier onboarding — the initial catalogue is built and curated directly by the founder, not an open marketplace suppliers join themselves
- Expansion beyond Nigeria — West Africa broadly is the next phase, not this one

## Vision

If this works, integrator becomes the default way systems integrators in Nigeria — and then West Africa — source, price, and deliver a job: the wholesale catalogue expands from the founder's own initial contacts into a genuinely broad multi-supplier marketplace, the loyalty program becomes a real reason integrators consolidate their buying in one place, and the platform's own trading history becomes the trust and credit signal the region currently lacks — the foundation for extending real trade credit or financing to integrators down the line, the way TradeDepot and Omnibiz have done in FMCG.

From there, the vertical widens deliberately, not accidentally: industrial automation and the complex commercial projects (multi-resident buildings, hotels, office complexes) that the founder already touches as an integrator become a natural second act, built once the core distribution and design model is proven on the simpler residential/light-commercial wedge. And the platform's reach could eventually extend past the integrator entirely — the parked idea of end clients discovering and hiring an integrator directly through the platform is the natural next surface once there's a real base of trusted, verified integrators to point clients toward.

None of that is required for this to succeed — success is a Nigerian integrator who buys again next month because this was genuinely better than what they were doing before. Everything past that is what a good first version earns the right to build.
